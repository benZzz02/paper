\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_data}

We evaluate SurgAlign with one central question: can a weak interval serve as a noisy temporal anchor more effectively than as a uniformly positive clip, and if so, on which tasks does that matter most? Because the claim of the paper is about the training rule rather than the backbone, the primary comparison is against a \emph{matched baseline} with identical data, encoders, frame budget, optimizer, and contrastive objective. SurgAlign differs only in how weak temporal supervision is consumed. In the primary setting, we use a LemonFM-initialized visual encoder, a SurgicBERTa text encoder, 8 sampled frames, 2 GPUs with per-GPU batch size 128, AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ and weight decay $0.02$, xpool-style frame selection, interval expansion ratio $1.5$, selection-loss weight $0.7$, hierarchical-consistency weight $0.1$, and mixed-level batches with a fine/mid/coarse ratio of $80/32/16$. During training, we sample from an expanded temporal window, compute text-guided frame weights, and optimize both the global and selected branches. Test-time evaluation uses standard CLIP-style video and text encoding.

Our downstream evaluation follows the standard zero-shot surgical recognition setup and covers phase, step, action, tool, and triplet prediction. This task diversity is important for our hypothesis: if temporal denoising improves the use of local evidence, the gains should be clearest on tasks that depend on fine-grained temporal cues rather than only broad workflow context.

We organize the experiments around two questions that are already supported by completed results. First, does SurgAlign improve zero-shot performance in the matched surgical-domain setting? Second, when it helps, is the gain concentrated on tasks that depend on local visual evidence rather than only broad procedural context? We also report full SurgCLIP as a stronger reference point, but that comparison should be interpreted separately because it changes more than the proposed training rule.

Relative to SurgCLIP-$\beta$, SurgAlign improves several zero-shot benchmarks, including Cholec80 Phase ($+4.75$ Acc / $+4.04$ F1), HeiChole Phase ($+3.47$ Acc / $+3.42$ F1), GraSP Phase ($+1.19$ Acc / $+4.31$ F1), GraSP Step ($+5.43$ Acc / $+5.27$ F1), SARRARP50 Action ($+1.22$ Acc / $+0.05$ F1), and GraSP Tool ($+1.16$ mAP). The strongest gain appears on GraSP Step, which is precisely the kind of evidence-sensitive task for which the proposed training rule is intended. This supports the core claim of the paper: when the relevant evidence is short and visually localized, weak interval pairs are better treated as noisy temporal anchors than as uniform positives.

The comparison with full SurgCLIP is more mixed. SurgAlign does not consistently surpass that stronger reference, and broad phase recognition remains challenging on datasets such as StrasBypass70 and BernBypass70. We therefore read the table conservatively: better positive construction targets a real bottleneck in weak surgical VLP, but temporal misalignment is not the only bottleneck.

\subsection{Where It Helps and Where It Does Not}
\label{sec:exp_scope}

The pattern of gains is as important as their average magnitude. SurgAlign helps most when the downstream task depends on short local evidence within repetitive context. GraSP Step is the clearest example, and the gains on GraSP Phase, Cholec80 Phase, and HeiChole Phase suggest that even phase recognition can benefit when phase boundaries are visually tied to local tool-tissue events. These are exactly the cases where uniform temporal pooling is most likely to dilute the positive signal.

The same table also clarifies the boundary of the method. Gains are not uniform on long-horizon phase benchmarks such as StrasBypass70 and BernBypass70, and they remain mixed on triplet and tool mAP. We interpret this pattern as consistent with the inductive bias of SurgAlign: the method helps when the paired text is semantically useful but temporally diluted, and helps less when the dominant challenge is broader workflow context, harder negative discrimination, or factors beyond local evidence selection.

The comparison against full SurgCLIP further sharpens this interpretation. SurgAlign narrows the gap through a better training rule, but does not by itself replace stronger end-to-end system design. This is the right scope for the paper: the contribution is a better way to consume weak interval-level positives, not a claim that temporal misalignment alone explains all remaining zero-shot errors.
