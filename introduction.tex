\section{Introduction}

Understanding surgical videos is important for a wide range of downstream applications, including workflow recognition, context-aware assistance, retrospective procedure analysis, surgical training, and automatic indexing of operative videos. However, traditional supervised learning in this domain depends heavily on dense expert annotations for phases, steps, tools, actions, and triplets, which are expensive to obtain, difficult to standardize across centers, and hard to scale to diverse procedures. These limitations make it difficult to build task-specific models for every workflow label space and every surgical domain, and they motivate learning transferable representations that can support zero-shot or low-supervision surgical evaluation. In particular, zero-shot surgical evaluation requires representations that capture semantically meaningful visual concepts beyond a fixed closed-set label space, so that knowledge learned from weak supervision can transfer across procedures, centers, and downstream tasks.

Against this background, surgical video-language pretraining (VLP) has emerged as a promising paradigm. Existing studies have shown that lecture videos, narrated procedures, and automatically constructed clip-caption pairs can provide scalable weak supervision for learning transferable workflow representations. Compared with training directly on task-specific labels, VLP offers a more scalable route to connect visual patterns with surgical semantics expressed in natural language, making it attractive for zero-shot transfer to phase, step, action, tool, and triplet recognition. More recent resources such as SurgLaVi further improve the scale, hierarchy, and curation quality of weakly supervised surgical video-text data, making CLIP-style pretraining increasingly practical for surgical understanding.

However, scalable weak supervision does not imply precise alignment. In most current CLIP-style surgical VLP pipelines, a weakly paired sample is constructed by associating a caption with a timestamped video interval, sampling multiple frames from that interval, aggregating them into a single clip embedding, and aligning that clip embedding with the caption through a standard contrastive objective. This formulation is simple and effective, but it also imposes a strong assumption: the sampled interval is treated as if it provided a uniformly reliable positive visual representation for the paired text.

That assumption is often too strong in surgical videos. In our setting, the language side is not a manually written caption produced after viewing a cleanly trimmed clip; it is typically derived from transcripts, narrations, or weak timestamped descriptions attached to a video interval. Such transcript-style supervision is naturally prone to temporal offset: speakers may describe an action before it becomes visually salient, comment on it after the key moment has passed, or summarize a broader procedural span while only a short local segment contains direct supporting evidence. Similar weak sequence alignment effects have been documented in instructional and narrated video settings, where language and visual actions are often only weakly synchronized rather than strictly timestamp-aligned~\cite{shen2021dwsa,jin2023ground_articles,xu2023procedure_aware_repr}. As a result, even when a weak clip-caption pair is globally valid, the visual evidence that directly supports the text is frequently concentrated in only a subset of local frames and may be slightly shifted relative to the annotated timestamp. This issue is particularly acute in surgery: transitions between phases are subtle, actions often unfold over short local spans, appearance differences across neighboring steps can be small, and textual descriptions are not always synchronized with the most informative visual moments. Treating the entire sampled interval as a homogeneous positive sample can therefore dilute the supervision signal and encourage alignment to contextual, transitional, or only weakly related content.

This mismatch matters because the downstream objectives of surgical VLP are not purely coarse-grained. Zero-shot phase recognition can sometimes benefit from broad contextual cues, but zero-shot step, action, tool, and especially triplet recognition require the model to associate text with more specific local evidence, such as short-lived instrument--action--target interactions. If weak supervision is consumed through uniformly pooled interval-level positives, the learned embedding may overemphasize procedure context and underemphasize the discriminative local evidence needed for fine-grained transfer. In other words, the problem is not only annotation noise; it is that the current training assumption can systematically bias the representation toward coarse workflow priors rather than the local surgical semantics that zero-shot evaluation ultimately demands.

SurgLaVi-style hierarchy is still informative for this problem, but mainly as an evidence-granularity prior rather than as a separate training objective. Fine-, mid-, and coarse-level annotations correspond to different temporal scopes: fine captions are shorter and more local, whereas mid/coarse captions summarize broader procedural context. This means the denoising mechanism itself should not behave identically across levels. Fine supervision should encourage sharper frame concentration, while broader captions should permit smoother evidence distributions.

These observations suggest a more precise formulation of the problem. The central issue is \emph{intra-sample temporal imprecision}: a weak video-text pair may be semantically correct in the large while still containing only a limited subset of genuinely supportive frames, and these frames may lie slightly before or after the annotated temporal boundary. From this perspective, the key modeling question is no longer only whether a caption matches an interval globally, but also how to denoise temporally imprecise positives while respecting the fact that different annotation levels imply different evidence granularity.

This way of formulating the problem is not ad hoc. Recent work on weakly supervised temporal localization and multiple-instance learning argues that coarse supervision should be treated as a partially positive bag rather than a uniformly reliable unit, and that latent evidence can be recovered through soft relevance weighting~\cite{dietterich1997mil,ilse2018attentionmil,li2023boostingwtal,hu2024mmplateau,li2024fps}. In text-video retrieval, text-conditioned aggregation methods such as X-Pool show that fixed mean pooling can encode irrelevant visual content and that frame contributions can instead be reweighted according to cross-modal relevance~\cite{gorti2022xpool}; more structured alignment models such as ProST further argue that language usually corresponds to local spatio-temporal evidence rather than a uniformly informative clip representation~\cite{li2023prost}. More broadly, CLIP-based video modeling has highlighted temporal context as a core challenge rather than a secondary detail~\cite{liu2023stan}, while temporal grounding methods show that fixed or overly narrow temporal scopes can fail to cover the true supporting moment under alignment uncertainty~\cite{pan2023scanning,huang2023wstsg}. Hierarchical surgical datasets such as SurgLaVi further suggest that temporal granularity varies across fine-, mid-, and coarse-level captions~\cite{surglavi2025}. Our method adapts these ideas to surgical VLP in a deliberately lightweight way.

Motivated by this view, we study weakly supervised surgical VLP through the lens of \emph{train-time temporal denoising}. Instead of trusting the annotated interval uniformly, we expand it into a small local neighborhood and let the paired text softly reweight candidate frames inside the expanded window. This produces a denoised selected view that is trained jointly with a standard global view. We further modulate the sharpness of frame selection using the annotation level, so that fine supervision encourages more concentrated evidence while broader captions allow smoother evidence distributions. In the hierarchical setting, we additionally encourage selected views from same-video fine/mid/coarse samples to remain cross-level consistent, so that hierarchy acts not only as a granularity prior but also as a lightweight structural constraint during training. Because these changes only affect train-time frame aggregation and auxiliary regularization, the method can still be formulated as a general training strategy for CLIP-style surgical VLP rather than as a backbone-specific design.

This design complements rather than replaces existing surgical VLP pipelines. We do not argue against hierarchical supervision, stronger backbones, or larger datasets. Instead, we argue that existing surgical VLP has largely improved weak supervision through better data and better encoders, while leaving the \emph{temporal imprecision inside weakly paired intervals} itself under-modeled. Our goal is therefore to improve how weak supervision is trusted: from uniform interval-level pooling to train-time temporal denoising, while preserving standard CLIP-style test-time inference. In the experiments, we therefore evaluate the method in two stages: first on a controlled CLIP baseline built from a surgery-domain-pretrained visual backbone, and then on public surgical VLP initializations retrained on SurgLaVi under the same denoising rule.

In summary, this work makes three contributions. First, we identify \emph{temporal imprecision inside weakly paired intervals} as a central and under-modeled issue in surgical VLP. Second, we propose a train-time window denoising framework that expands each weak interval into a local neighborhood, uses paired text to softly reweight candidate frames, and introduces lightweight cross-level consistency between selected views from same-video hierarchical samples, thereby constructing denoised visual training views without changing the test-time inference path. Third, we validate the method in both a controlled setting built from a surgery-domain-pretrained visual backbone and a broader setting that adapts public surgical VLP initializations on SurgLaVi, showing that the same denoising rule is effective beyond a single backbone instantiation.
